Motion & Forces

Motion and forces are some of the most important ideas in physics. They help us understand how objects move, why they move, and what causes their motion to change.


Understanding Motion

Motion occurs when an object's position changes over time. To describe motion, physicists use quantities such as distance, displacement, speed, velocity, and acceleration.


Distance and Displacement

Distance is the total amount of ground an object travels. Displacement describes the change in an object's position from its starting point to its final position and includes direction.


Speed and Velocity

Speed describes how quickly an object moves, while velocity describes both how quickly an object moves and the direction of its motion.


Average speed can be calculated by dividing the total distance traveled by the total time.


Acceleration

Acceleration describes how quickly velocity changes. An object accelerates when it speeds up, slows down, or changes direction.


Acceleration is measured in meters per second squared (m/s²).


Forces

A force is a push or pull that can change an object's motion. Forces are measured in newtons (N). Forces can cause an object to accelerate, slow down, change direction, or remain at rest.


Common Forces

Gravity is the attractive force between objects with mass. Near Earth's surface, gravity causes objects to accelerate downward.


Friction is a force that opposes motion between surfaces. The normal force acts perpendicular to a surface, while tension can occur in ropes, cables, or strings.


Newton's Laws of Motion


Newton's First Law

An object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion continues moving at constant velocity unless acted upon by a net external force. This idea is known as inertia.


Newton's Second Law

The acceleration of an object depends on the net force acting on it and its mass. A greater net force produces greater acceleration, while a greater mass makes the same force produce less acceleration.


The relationship is commonly written as F = ma.


Newton's Third Law

When one object exerts a force on another object, the second object exerts a force of equal magnitude in the opposite direction on the first object. These forces act on different objects.


Momentum

Momentum describes the quantity of motion an object has. It depends on both the object's mass and velocity. A heavier or faster-moving object has greater momentum.


In an isolated system, total momentum is conserved. This principle is especially useful when studying collisions and interactions between objects.


Gravity

Gravity is a force that attracts objects toward one another. On Earth, gravity gives falling objects an acceleration of approximately 9.8 m/s² when air resistance is ignored.


Key Ideas

Motion tells us how an object's position changes. Forces explain why an object's motion changes. Together, these concepts allow physicists to analyze everything from a falling ball to a moving vehicle, spacecraft, and planets.
